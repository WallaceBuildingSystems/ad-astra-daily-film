# Kary Mullis, the polymerase chain reaction (research, 19 October 2026, James's session)

Selection test: 1 One person owns the idea (Mullis, Nobel Prize 1993, half share). 2 Zero to one: no way to copy a chosen stretch of DNA. 3 Changed how we live: diagnosis, forensics, virus tests. 4 Lasting: every genetics lab runs it.

## Facts used, with sources
- 1983, Cetus Corporation, Emeryville, California; the idea came on a night drive. Sources: Embryo Project, Arizona State University https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/polymerase-chain-reaction-pcr ; Wikipedia. The road differs by source, so the film names none.
- Steps: heat to 95 degrees separates the strands, cool lets starters (primers) bind, enzyme builds new strands. Each round doubles the DNA; 30 rounds give 2 to the power 30, 1,073,741,824 copies. Sources: Embryo Project; Wikipedia.
- Early on the enzyme was added fresh each round because heat destroys it; a heat-tolerant enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus let machines run the rounds (1988 paper, Saiki and colleagues). Sources: Embryo Project; Wikipedia. The year Taq was adopted and its discovery date differ by source (1966, 1976), so the film gives none.
- 1985 first paper (Saiki, Mullis, Erlich, sickle cell anemia). Sources: Embryo Project; Wikipedia.
- 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, half to Mullis, half to Michael Smith. Source: nobelprize.org https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1993/mullis/facts/
- Uses: virus detection including COVID tests, forensic DNA analysis. Source: Embryo Project.
- Not used: the Roche price, the 1989 award, the road, the month, what colleagues said.

Copying DNA began on a night drive.
California, 1983.
Kary Mullis, a chemist at Cetus, saw how to copy one piece of DNA.
Heat splits the two strands.
Cool it, and short starters attach.
An enzyme builds the missing halves.
Repeat, and the copies double.
Thirty rounds make over 1 billion copies.
Early on, the heat killed the enzyme, so it was added fresh each round.
A heat-loving bacterium's enzyme survived, and machines ran the rounds alone.
1985, the method was published.
1993, Mullis shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
Every virus test and forensic DNA match starts with it.
